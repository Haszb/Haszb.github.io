## The question

Many Bitcoin forecasting examples predict one step at a time, then feed the model the actual price before making the next prediction. This evaluates repeated short-term forecasts, not how a model behaves when it must rely on its own predictions. This project asked a harder question: **can BTC/USDT price history alone support a useful multi-step forecast when the model has to rely on its own predictions?**

## The data

The first step was an automated pipeline to collect hourly candles from Binance, followed by an exploratory analysis of the market from January 2022 to October 2025. Beyond price, the analysis covered volumes, trade counts, buyer and seller pressure, trade-size distributions and normalised volatility.

The analysis also revealed a market mostly driven by small trades, with relatively few signs of large individual executions on this platform, and a recurring yearly rhythm in trade sizes. It also found that **2025 diverges from the pattern observed in the previous years**. The usual seasonality disappears, and trades get smaller even as the price climbs. That break was the reason the models were trained on 2025 data only.

## The experiment

The models were recurrent neural networks (LSTM, GRU and SimpleRNN), evaluated with **autoregressive walk-forward forecasting**. Each model received an initial window of observed prices (24 hours by default). After that initial window, every forecast depended on the model's own previous predictions rather than newly observed prices. This exposed the models to the error accumulation they would face in a real multi-step forecast. The data was split chronologically to prevent information leakage, and random seeds were fixed to improve reproducibility.

Aggregate metrics such as MAE and RMSE do not show how a multi-step forecast evolves over time. A prediction can appear close to the observed price by luck or just by following the general trend. The error was therefore also tracked at each step (absolute percentage error), and every run was inspected visually.

To rule out a bad configuration, seven hyperparameters were varied one at a time (input window, batch size, learning rate, units, dropout, depth and loss function) across the three architectures, first on raw prices and then on log-returns, which are more stationary. In total **168 runs were logged in MLflow**. A Dash dashboard was built to compare them side by side. The interactive version below lets you explore the exported results directly in your browser.

## The result

**None of the 168 runs produced a forecast reliable enough to be useful.**

- **LSTM** models consistently drifted towards relatively flat predictions, failing to capture subsequent price movements.
- **GRU** models showed similar behaviour in many runs. Others generated oscillations that resembled market movements but largely reproduced patterns from the input sequence.
- **SimpleRNN** models displayed the widest range of behaviours, from repeated patterns to implausible trajectories, without producing a convincing forecast.

Switching from raw prices to log-returns made the series more stationary but did not change the conclusion. Some forecasts flattened towards zero; others reproduced oscillatory patterns without reliably tracking the market.

The experiments did not establish that Bitcoin prices are inherently unpredictable. They showed something narrower, but useful: these architectures and configurations did not extract a robust forecasting signal from the available price history under recursive evaluation.

## Why it led to Rochondra

Price is an outcome, not a complete explanation of the forces behind it. Its history reflects the combined effects of information, market participants and changing conditions, many of which are not directly observable in the price series itself.

After 168 runs, there was no convincing evidence that this approach could produce useful forecasts. Adding external variables was a possible next step, but it would have turned the project into a different problem, in an already crowded field.

Instead, the project went in a different direction: studying the inputs and decisions that shape an outcome, rather than trying to predict it.

That idea became Rochondra: an attempt to understand projects through the traces they leave as they are built, starting with their whitepapers. The decision is recorded as the first entry in Rochondra's [decision log](https://github.com/Haszb/Rochondra/blob/main/docs/decision-log.md#d1-measuring-inputs-instead-of-predicting-price), and the full reasoning is explained in [Why I stopped predicting Bitcoin prices and chose a new direction](https://medium.com/@rochondra.lab/a-new-direction-89003b3017d9).

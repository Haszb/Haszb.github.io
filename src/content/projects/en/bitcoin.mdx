## The question

Many Bitcoin forecasting examples predict one step at a time, then feed the model the actual price before computing the next prediction. They evaluate a series of successive short-term forecasts, but do not reproduce how a model behaves when it must rely on its own predictions. This project asks a harder question: **does BTC/USDT price history alone contain a signal that can forecast several steps ahead, when the model can only rely on its own predictions?**

## The data

The work began with an automated pipeline that collects hourly candles from Binance, followed by an exploratory analysis of the market between January 2022 and October 2025. Beyond price, the analysis looked at volumes, trade counts, buyer and seller pressure, trade-size distributions and normalised volatility.

The analysis also showed a market mostly driven by small trades, with relatively few signs of large individual trades on the platform. It revealed a yearly seasonality in trade sizes too, and an **apparent break in 2025: the pattern of the previous years disappears**, and trades get smaller even as the price climbs.

These observations concern the Binance data studied and cannot, on their own, describe the Bitcoin market as a whole.

That break led to one constraint: training the models on 2025 data only.

## The experiment

The models tested were three recurrent neural-network architectures: LSTM, GRU and SimpleRNN, evaluated with a recursive *walk-forward* forecasting strategy.

Each model received an initial window of observed prices (24 hours by default). Beyond it, every forecast depended on the model's own previous predictions rather than on newly observed data. This shows how errors accumulate as the model moves away from known data.

The data was split chronologically to prevent information leakage between training and test, and random seeds were also fixed to improve reproducibility.

/>

Aggregate metrics such as MAE and RMSE are not enough to show how a recursive forecast evolves over time. An overall error can hide a gradual drift, artificial oscillations, or a forecast that stays close to the price through simple inertia, as in the example above.

The absolute percentage error was therefore also tracked at each step, and the results of every run were inspected visually.

To rule out a bad configuration, seven hyperparameters were varied one at a time: input window size, batch size, learning rate, units, dropout, network depth and loss function.

The three architectures were tested on raw prices, then on log-returns, a representation that can make non-stationary financial series easier to model.

In total, **168 runs were logged in MLflow**. A Dash dashboard was built to compare them side by side.

## The result

**None of the 168 runs produced a forecast reliable enough to be useful.**

- **LSTM** models consistently drifted towards relatively flat predictions, failing to capture subsequent price movements.
- **GRU** models showed similar behaviour in many runs. In others, they produced oscillations that resembled market movements but largely reproduced the pattern of the input sequence.
- **SimpleRNN** models displayed the widest range of behaviours, from repeated patterns to implausible trajectories, without producing a convincing forecast.

Switching from raw prices to log-returns made the series more stationary but did not change the conclusion. Some forecasts converged towards zero; others reproduced oscillations without reliably tracking the market.

The experiments did not establish that Bitcoin prices are inherently unpredictable. More precisely, they show that the architectures, configurations and data tested did not extract a robust forecasting signal under this recursive evaluation.

## Why this experiment led to Rochondra

Price is an outcome, not a complete explanation of the forces behind it. Its history reflects the combined effects of information, market participants and changing conditions, many of which are not directly observable in the price series itself.

After 168 runs, there was no convincing evidence that this approach could produce useful forecasts. Adding external variables was a relevant lead, but also a change of scope, in an already crowded research field.

Another option was to change the question: rather than trying to predict an asset's price, study the projects behind it.

That idea became Rochondra: a method for understanding projects through the traces they leave as they develop, starting with their whitepapers. The decision is recorded as the first entry in Rochondra's [decision log](https://github.com/Haszb/Rochondra/blob/main/docs/decision-log.md#d1-measuring-inputs-instead-of-predicting-price), and the full reasoning is explained in [Why I stopped predicting Bitcoin prices and chose a new direction](https://medium.com/@rochondra.lab/a-new-direction-89003b3017d9).
